# September 20: full-ring I/O, simpler ownership, and eager acceptance

[History index](README.md)

The next objective was to stop obtaining the headline gains by bypassing io_uring. Direct accept, receive, and send were disabled for the measured full-ring comparisons. The original single-pump Kestrel architecture and real HTTP processing remained.

## Preparing SQEs on caller threads was a large regression

The first submission rewrite replaced a pending-operation dictionary with generation-tagged slots and prepared SQEs directly from calling threads under a shared lock, queueing only overflow.

| Workload | Previous full-ring median RPS | Caller-thread SQ median RPS | Change |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Keep-alive, 64 connections | 149,817 | 52,913 | -64.7% |
| Short, 16 connections | 16,580 | 11,107 | -33.0% |

Diagnostics showed much smaller completion batches and more wakeups per send. Removing one completion lock did not recover the loss. Returning SQ preparation and submission to the pump, with typed reusable commands from other threads, restored the prior throughput.

The final typed-queue/slot comparison was effectively tied with the preceding full-ring implementation: about +0.15% keep-alive and -0.73% short. Close allocation fell about 104 bytes/request. Fewer dictionaries and more direct submission did not automatically mean less total scheduling/synchronization work.

## Queue batching and combined submit/wait did not earn their complexity

Two further alternatives were independently tested: swapping a shared command queue into a pump-local batch, and using combined `io_uring_submit_and_wait` instead of separate submission/collection. Neither showed a consistent matched-round throughput, CPU, or latency improvement. The combined configuration's favorable aggregate median was misleading because the unchanged controls drifted substantially during the run order.

The simpler pump-owned action queue and pending dictionary were therefore restored as the default. Reusable completion state remained, preserving the earlier major allocation reduction. Slot-based submission remained an experiment, saving roughly 107 bytes per short request in the final comparison but not materially improving RPS.

This is distinct from eventfd wake coalescing: changing how the pump acquires/stages a batch and avoiding redundant producer notifications are different mechanisms.

## Eager accept was the important full-ring improvement

The old demand-driven accept waited for Kestrel to construct a connection and ask for another. Two new modes shared a bounded accepted-handle channel:

- Eager one-shot accept rearmed from the pump, without waiting for application-side consumption.
- Multishot accept used the same queue and ownership, but kept one accept submission active across many connections.

| Plaintext short workload | Original demand-driven RPS | Eager one-shot RPS | Multishot RPS |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| 16 connections, three-run medians | 15,964 | 30,785 (+93%) | 31,050 (+95%) |
| 64 connections, separate three-run matrix | 13,729 | 43,325 | 37,723 |

Most of the first improvement was eager acceptance, not multishot specifically. At the higher concurrency, multishot was approximately 12-14% slower than eager one-shot in each matched round.

Mechanism checks were explicit: one multishot diagnostic delivered 207,358 successful accepts from one SQE and eventually one terminal accept CQE. Cancel acknowledgment did not substitute for original-operation completion. Queue high/low thresholds paused and resumed acceptance while keeping descriptor ownership bounded.

## Deferred task work provided another measured gain

The ring was enabled on its owning pump with single-issuer/deferred-task-work settings, letting that thread drive the deferred completion work. This was not SQPOLL or busy spinning.

| Same accept mode, normal -> deferred task work | Normal RPS | Deferred RPS | Approximate median change |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Eager one-shot, keep-alive | 135,789 | 142,299 | +4.8% |
| Eager one-shot, short | 44,528 | 47,979 | +7.7% |
| Multishot, keep-alive | 134,427 | 144,426 | +7.4% |
| Multishot, short | 39,974 | 42,466 | +6.2% |

Throughput and CPU/request improved in every matched round for both accept modes. Tail latency was not uniformly better. The retained older-backend configuration selected eager one-shot acceptance, deferred task work, and the simpler pump-owned queue/dictionary. Multishot remained selectable rather than being silently replaced.

## Evidence and boundaries

See the [original sample report](../../../../samples/NetworkProtoSample/RESULTS.md), particularly `Full-ring submission experiment`, `Pump alternatives`, and `Full-ring receive, eager accept and multishot`.

The local `results/submission`, `results/pump-alternatives`, and `results/multishot` groups record September 20 timestamps. The measurements used monotonic-clock wrk 4.1.0, separate ten-second warmup and measurement, four server cores, usually two client cores and occasionally four for higher-concurrency close.

Tiny-ring overflow, partial sends, terminal cancellation, accepted-handle backpressure, real HTTP/HTTPS basic behavior, and server EOF were exercised. Receive and send were still one-shot with pinned pipe buffers; this was accept multishot, not the later provided-page multishot receive. Keep-alive could still saturate the single pump while leaving other server cores underused. The older strict HTTPS close-load warning issue was not claimed fixed.
