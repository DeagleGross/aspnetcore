# October 1: direct epoll output and a scaling limit

[History index](README.md)

The goal was to focus on the TCP/fd-TLS epoll prototypes without designing the future layered abstraction or changing io_uring. The remembered TLS advantage was real: an earlier matched C# epoll run was about 27% above its own stock baseline, whereas later cross-date tables diluted that percentage.

## What changed

The existing `Engine` was already an epoll-specific worker, not a shared io_uring engine. Renaming it alone would not optimize anything. An initial reusable-send-completion/direct-reference attempt gave mixed or worse results and was superseded.

The retained implementation is `EpollWorker`, with direct connection references for queued work and output-pipe draining on the worker itself. The old ThreadPool output loop, queued send, per-send completion task, and completion-to-ThreadPool hop are gone. A pipe reader notification only enqueues output-ready work; it can run inline without running HTTP application code on the worker. Pending output memory remains pinned through real partial sends/TLS retries. Successful synchronous output continues on the worker within a bounded budget.

TCP additionally avoids an immediate usually-empty recv after a short successful read; level-triggered readiness still reports further data or EOF. The full TCP/TLS state machines, lifecycle bookkeeping, native syscall/OpenSSL shim, and epoll-local state contract remain. No new universal transport interface, custom BIO layer, application shortcut, or io_uring change was made.

## Matched results

The pre-change build was preserved. Each final cell below is a two-run mean with reversed configuration order. Four/eight server cores also mean four/eight .NET processors and epoll workers. The client used eight separate physical cores, 1,200 connections, 1 KiB real Kestrel HTTP/1.1 responses, three-second warmup, one-second settle and 15-second measurement. All experimental runs used coalesced wakes and final-send batching.

| Server cores | Implementation | TCP short RPS | TCP long RPS | TLS short RPS | TLS long RPS |
|---|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 4 | Stock | 67,614 | 247,507 | 4,843 | 153,019 |
| 4 | Previous epoll | 94,556 | 249,329 | 8,250 | 176,220 |
| 4 | Direct-output epoll | 103,190 | 281,038 | 8,268 | 178,823 |
| 8 | Stock | 58,447 | 502,467 | 8,647 | 300,411 |
| 8 | Previous epoll | 143,128 | 478,321 | 15,610 | 364,614 |
| 8 | Direct-output epoll | 147,079 | 483,249 | 15,769 | 366,683 |

At four cores, TCP improved approximately 9% short / 13% long over previous epoll; TLS changed roughly 0% / 1.5%. At eight cores, gains over previous epoll were small (approximately 0.6-2.8%). Epoll TLS long remained about 22% above fresh stock at eight cores, but most of that advantage predated the output rewrite. Stock beat epoll TCP long at eight cores by about 4%.

Allocation did not universally improve: four-core persistent allocation rose roughly 3.6 -> 3.9 KiB/request for TCP and 3.3 -> 4.1 KiB for TLS, while stock remained about 1.6 KiB. The direct path prioritizes throughput and removes scheduling machinery; it is not a zero-allocation or unconditional efficiency win.

## Scaling separately from changing the application budget

Increasing both cores/workers from 1 -> 2 -> 4 -> 8 gave TCP long roughly 34k -> 129k -> 281k -> 483k and TLS long 24k -> 86k -> 179k -> 367k. The low-core cells were screens rather than repeated estimates.

A separate fixed-eight-core process test varied only workers. TCP long was roughly 92k / 225k / 397k / 483k at 1/2/4/8 workers; TLS was 49k / 112k / 274k / 377k. The single TLS worker averaged about 92% CPU, supporting a worker serialization limit when too few workers serve a larger application CPU budget.

Changing concurrency did not produce a stable higher universal result. TCP at 600 connections confirmed about 484k for new epoll versus 500k old and 529k stock. TLS at 120 connections reached 386k once but later varied 322k-379k, with similar drift in controls. At 4,800 connections both protocols got slower. Eight server/eight client physical cores is the largest disjoint arrangement measured.

These are overloaded achieved rates, not sustainable service rates or a physical-NIC maximum. TLS short used about 7.4 of the client's eight cores, so it cannot establish the server's independent handshake ceiling. Stock TCP short had substantial timeout counts, especially at eight cores; its low short RPS is not an intrinsic eight-core limit.

## Evidence and checks

All epoll load runs had complete worker/connection/page accounting, zero TIME_WAIT-overflow deltas, and no wrk socket/HTTP error summary. Peer disconnect counters remain recorded. Real TCP partial sends, half-close, cancelled reads, slow readers, fragmented requests, TLS retries, close-notify, idle-handshake deadlines and late accepts passed the existing sanitizer checks. Output modes 0-3 were also checked with eight workers.

The [epoll report](../../Epoll/README.md#direct-worker-output-and-scaling-october-1) contains exact tables, methodology, CPU/allocation observations, failed/unsuccessful candidate history and reproduction commands. Sample `results/epoll-tuning-20261001.json` indexes 136 labeled measurements without pooling different candidate implementations or baselines. No additional sessions/agents, commits, pushes, or io_uring modifications were used.
